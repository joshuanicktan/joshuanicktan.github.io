  • Holds the 7 kN VdS 2597 lock load with a safety factor of 1.4 in FEA

What I did

  • Designed the ratchet-and-pawl lock: waterjet-cut 6061 aluminium that grips poles up to 50 mm and stays locked if the electronics fail
  • Ran FEA on the lock at the 7 kN load from the VdS 2597 lock guideline: safety factor 1.4
  • Made the mould and laid up the six-layer carbon fibre shell, vacuum bagged
  • Debugged the electronics and integrated three controller boards into the lock case

The problem

The locks we timed took 26 s on average to lock, bent over the rack, and 70% of the 37 cyclists we surveyed go back to check their lock.

Team

Team of 6: 2 mechanical, 1 embedded, 1 UI/UX, 1 mobile app, 1 electronics and PM

Locking a bike usually means bending over the rack and fiddling with a key, and lending it means lending the key too. For SUTD’s 30.007 Engineering Design Innovation module (theme: the travel experience), our team of six designed Click&Go: a lock on the front fork that clicks onto a pole when you push the bike in, and unlocks with the app, a key fob or a fingerprint. We aimed for a product that could stand next to commercial locks, not a class prototype.

Overview

Why

Section titled “Why”

We surveyed 37 cyclists, and parking was the problem they raised most. 70% go back to check their lock after locking it, and the most common rating for the effort of locking was 2 out of 5. We then timed the three locks they used most: U, cable and folding locks took 26 seconds on average to lock and 19 to unlock.

That set the brief:

  • Lock and unlock in under 20 seconds.
  • Fit the poles and railings bikes get locked to, up to 50 mm across.
  • Match the security guideline commercial locks follow (VdS 2597).

How you use it

Section titled “How you use it”
  • ⤢

    1. Push the bike onto a pole or rail: the lock clicks shut.

  • ⤢

    2. Unlock from the app, online or over Bluetooth.

  • ⤢

    3. Or press the radio key fob.

  • ⤢

    4. Or put your thumb on the fingerprint sensor on the handlebar.

Stills from our demo video, styled for the project poster.

How it works

Section titled “How it works”

The lock rides on the front fork. Push the bike onto a pole and the ratchet finger clicks shut around it, and a limit switch tells the electronics the bike is locked. To unlock, use any one of the three: the app (over Bluetooth or the internet), the radio key fob, or the fingerprint sensor on the handlebar. If someone tampers with a locked bike, a vibration sensor sets off an 89 dB alarm, and the app shows where the bike is by GPS.

What users told us

Section titled “What users told us”

User feedback showed what mattered beyond convenience: people needed to feel that the lock had engaged. That confirmation was crucial to them on top of the speed, the app and every other convenience feature, and the ratchet and pawl gives it as a tactile click.

Part 3 of 7My work

Locking mechanism and FEA

I designed the lock itself: the part that has to click shut when you push the bike onto a pole, hold when someone pulls, and let go only when it’s told to.

How it locks

Section titled “How it locks”

The finger that hooks around the pole has an arc on its outer edge, centred off the pivot. A pole pushed in from outside makes a moment about the pivot and swings the finger open. Once the pole is past, springs close the finger and the pawl drops into the ratchet: that’s the click. The inner edge works the other way round. Pulled outwards, it releases the pole only when the lock is unlocked, and holds it when locked. With many teeth on the ratchet, the finger closes snugly on any pole up to 50 mm across.

Locked unless told otherwise

Section titled “Locked unless told otherwise”

The servo only lifts the pawl to unlock; it never holds the lock shut. Linear springs do that, so a flat battery or a fault leaves the bike locked rather than open.

The lock plates were rough cut on a waterjet from 10 mm 6061 aluminium to save machining time, then milled and tapped where needed.

Strength against the lock guideline

Section titled “Strength against the lock guideline”

Bike locks are tested to the VdS 2597 guideline, which asks for 7 kN in the locking cylinder.

  • Hand calculation: shearing the ratchet teeth (150 mm² at aluminium’s 207 MPa shear strength) would take 31 kN.
  • FEA: at 7 kN, with material properties from our own tensile tests, the safety factor in the locking cylinder is 1.4. That’s a simulation, not the guideline’s physical test.

The mount and pivot

Section titled “The mount and pivot”

The case mounts on the front fork with a black anodised bracket, held by two steel cable-reinforced cable-tie locks, so it fits any ordinary frame. A spring-loaded pivot lets the whole case tilt to match the angle of the pole, then brings it back level when the bike moves off.

In the 3D model, “Finger (the hook)” swings the finger open as a pole would, “Case tilt” tips the case on its pivot, and “Take it apart” goes step by step from the bike down to these plates, with a label on each piece.

Electronics and app

My part here was debugging the boards and fitting them into the lock case.

Three controllers

Section titled “Three controllers”
  • ESP32 with a 4G SIM (LILYGO T-SIM): the internet link to the app’s database, and GPS.
  • A second ESP32: Bluetooth to the phone and LoRa radio to the key fob. We added it because the 4G board couldn’t run the SIM and Bluetooth together, and was short of serial ports.
  • Arduino Nano: the fingerprint sensor, a vibration sensor, the limit switch that detects locking, the servo that releases the ratchet, and the alarm, switched through a MOSFET.

An 18650 cell keeps the GPS running if the main battery runs out. The key fob is a fourth, smaller board: an ESP32 with its own LoRa radio and two buttons.

One pipeline for every way in

Section titled “One pipeline for every way in”

A request from any source passes down the chain to the Nano, which changes the lock and replies with the new lock and alarm state. If the limit switch or the alarm fires, that state goes back up to the app. Any one way unlocks it: the app, the key fob or a fingerprint. The app’s online unlock works from anywhere, so you can lend the bike to a friend without handing over a key.

Fitting it in

Section titled “Fitting it in”

What we measured:

  • The electronics kept working through bumps of about 13.8 m/s² on a test ride.
  • Bluetooth reached 100 m in line of sight, and the key fob at least 100 m.
  • The alarm measured 89.3 dB switched through the MOSFET, against 93.3 dB wired straight to power.

The app

Section titled “The app”

The app runs on Android, built in Unity with its interface as a web page inside it (Leaflet for the map) and Firebase holding each lock’s state. A Telegram bot messages you when the bike locks or unlocks, or when the alarm goes off, which also leaves a history.

Part 5 of 7My work

Carbon fibre shell

I made the shell: the mould, the carbon fibre layup, and the split case the electronics go into.

Why carbon fibre

Section titled “Why carbon fibre”

The case has to protect the electronics if the bike falls or hits something, shrug off scratches from branches and railings, and resist basic hand tools. A carbon fibre and epoxy skin over a 3D-printed PLA inner shell does that without much weight.

The shape came from free-form design run through CFD. Drag turned out to be negligible whatever the shape, but users told us the look and perceived aerodynamics mattered to whether they’d use it, so we kept the streamlined form.

Layup

Section titled “Layup”

Over the mould, I laid up six layers of plain-weave carbon fibre by hand in a quasi-isotropic stack, then vacuum bagged it so the fibre and epoxy stayed compressed through about 24 hours of curing.

Assembly

Section titled “Assembly”

The shell splits in half so the electronics can go in. A tongue and groove lines the halves up and leaves a channel for a gasket, since bikes get rained on. The mounts for the electronics inside are 3D printed.

Testing the composite

Section titled “Testing the composite”

We cut samples from panels made the same way as the shell and tested them ourselves: tensile (ASTM D3039), three-point flexural (ASTM D7264) and Rockwell hardness (ASTM D785).

Property Our CF-epoxy 6061 aluminium (same tests)
Tensile yield strength 26.8 MPa 279 MPa
Flexural yield strength 41.9 to 67.7 MPa 277 MPa
Rockwell hardness 115 HRL 118 HRL
  • ⤢

    Tensile: our composite (grey) peaked near 27 MPa, close to PLA (blue); aluminium (orange) runs off the top of the scale.

  • ⤢

    Flexural: the composite reached 68 MPa with the carbon face up and 42 MPa face down; aluminium is the top curve.

  • ⤢

    Hardness: our composite next to aluminium; the third bar is a published value for epoxy.

Hardness matched aluminium, so knocks from branches and stones won’t mark the shell. Strength came out far below published values for carbon fibre and epoxy, most likely from how the epoxy was mixed, voids from uneven resin, and samples cut by hand with shears after the waterjet broke down. We used our measured properties, not datasheet values, in the shell’s impact FEA.

Results against requirements

We set every target from a measurement or a published standard, then checked the prototype against it.

Requirement Target Where it came from Result
Time to lock and unlock Under 20 s Timing U, cable and folding locks: 26 s to lock and 19 s to unlock on average 4 s to lock, 14 s to unlock
Effect on balance Small The lock hangs on one side of the fork 2.1 to 2.7% more energy, mostly from its 2.13 kg
Drag coefficient Under 1.13 A rider’s own drag coefficient (Chowdhury et al., 2011) 0.81 (CFD)
Pole diameter 50 mm Poles average 40 mm and railings 50 mm 50 mm
Load in the locking cylinder 7 kN The VdS 2597 lock guideline Safety factor 1.4 (FEA)
Shell hardness At least aluminium’s Knocks from branches, stones and railings 115 HRL against 118 HRL (surface hardness only; tensile strength is about a tenth of aluminium’s)

Time

Section titled “Time”

Unlocking takes longer than locking: in our timing, almost all of it was the fingerprint check. Both stay under the 20 s target.

Balance

Section titled “Balance”

Because the lock sits off-centre, we worked out the extra energy needed to ride and keep balance through turns, for a 60 kg rider on a 12 kg bike at 7 m/s. It came to 2.1% riding straight and 2.7% in the tightest turn, almost all from the lock’s mass rather than its offset.

Drag

Section titled “Drag”

A drag coefficient describes the shape, not the total drag, which also depends on frontal area. So this compares how streamlined the case is with a rider, not the force each adds. The case’s frontal area is far smaller than a rider’s, so its share of the total drag is smaller still.

Part 7 of 7

Lessons

What we learnt

Section titled “What we learnt”
  • People care how a lock feels. Speed was our headline, but users also needed to feel the lock engage, and the ratchet’s click gives them that.
  • Make test samples the way you make the part, and test them early. Our composite came out far weaker than published values, most likely from the epoxy mix and voids. Testing a sample panel before the real layup would have shown it sooner.
  • Reading a fingerprint takes time. Unlocking took 14 s against 4 s to lock, almost all of it the fingerprint check, so it’s the first place to look for a faster unlock. The app and the key fob each unlock it on their own.
  • Libraries set limits. The Bluetooth plugin for the app only supported Bluetooth Classic, which uses more power than Bluetooth Low Energy.

A next version

Section titled “A next version”
  • Smaller electronics: custom surface-mount boards shaped to the case would use today’s dead space, shrinking the lock to about 65% of its current volume.
  • Production materials: the fingerprint enclosure injection moulded in ABS for UV and weather resistance, with a silicone strap.
  • Beyond bikes: the same system on other mobility devices, the app as a base for bike sharing, and a smart luggage lock.
