The problem

Singapore imports over 90% of its food and wasted 774,000 tonnes of it in 2019. Dried food keeps far longer, but electric dryers cost power.

Team

Team of 5

For SUTD’s 10.023 Designing Energy Systems module, our team of five built a food dryer that runs on sunlight alone. Two parabolic mirrors focus the sun onto black aluminium tubes, a fan drives the air round a closed loop through the food and a desiccant, and a vent door lets the hot, humid air out when it passes the target. No heater, and nothing to pay to run it.

Overview

Why

Section titled “Why”

More than 90% of Singapore’s food is imported, and only about 1% of its land is set aside for farming; the government’s “30 by 30” goal is to produce 30% of the nation’s nutritional needs locally by 2030. Meanwhile Singapore wasted 774,000 tonnes of food in 2019. Fruit thrown out as it expires, at supermarkets and at home, is the easiest to save: dried, it keeps for months. So we set out to build a low-cost dryer for households and small food sellers that runs on sunlight.

The brief

Section titled “The brief”
  • Work in less than ideal sunlight.
  • Last at least 3 years.
  • Dry food quickly.
  • Run 12 hours a day.
  • No heating elements.
  • Hold the air at 45 to 60 °C, so the food keeps its quality.

How it works

Section titled “How it works”
  • The sun heats the tubes. Two parabolic mirrors, each 375 mm across, focus sunlight onto black-painted aluminium tubes. Aluminium is light and conducts heat well, so the tubes heat the air inside them quickly, and concentrating the light keeps it working when the sun is weak.
  • The air goes round a loop. A fan drives the air down one tube, through the food and a desiccant (silica gel) in the front pipe, back up the other tube and across the back pipe. Heating the same air again and again got it to 85 °C at peak sun.
  • A vent holds the temperature. When the air passes the target temperature or humidity, a vent door at one corner opens: the hot, humid air leaves and fresh air comes in.
  • The sun runs the fan too. A 10 W solar panel powers the fan and charges a battery that keeps it running for 2.5 days without sun.

We kept plastic out of the air’s path as far as we could, so the food isn’t exposed to fumes from it; the printed parts in a product would be low-VOC plastics.

Part 3 of 6My work

Mirrors, air loop and vent

Parabolic troughs

Section titled “Parabolic troughs”

A parabola reflects every ray that comes in along its axis to one point, its focus; stretched into a trough, that point becomes a line. I modelled the parabola parametrically in Fusion 360, so its width and focal length could change while the ribs, the tube and the mirror kept their places on it. Each trough is 375 mm across, with a 50 by 25 mm aluminium tube, painted black, along its focal line.

  • ⤢

    Three ribs hold each mirror to the curve; the ring carries the tube, and the feet bolt to the frame.

  • ⤢

    The mirror: reflective film glued by hand onto plastic film, then bent over the ribs.

With 0.42 m² of mirror, sunlight at 1,000 W/m² and half of it turned into heat, the troughs collect 420 Wh in 2 hours. Evaporating 160 g of water takes about 107 Wh (2,400 J/g), so the sun gives about four times what the drying needs. The rest could be stored for later in a heat battery (see Lessons).

A closed loop of air

Section titled “A closed loop of air”

The mirrors only heat what passes through their focal lines, so we sent the same air through them again and again. Air goes down one hot tube, through the fan, the desiccant and the food in the front pipe, back up the other tube and across the back pipe. At peak sun the air in the loop reached 85 °C.

That’s too hot for food, which keeps its quality at 45 to 60 °C. A vent door at the front corner, where the first tube turns into the front pipe, holds it there. The door is a curved wedge on a hinge at the corner. Shut, it closes six holes in the corner’s outer side and its open front, and its curved face turns the air from the tube into the fan. When the temperature or the humidity passes its target, it swings open: its inner side cuts across the corner and sends the hot, humid air from the tube out through the holes, while its front swings out and lets fresh air in to the fan.

    Vent shut, seen from above: the door (hatched blue) closes the side and the front, and its curve turns the air into the fan.

  • ⤢

    Vent open: the door turns the tube's hot air out of the side (pink), and fresh air comes in at the front (blue).

  • ⤢

    From outside, shut: the door covers the six holes from inside.

  • ⤢

    Open: hot air out through the holes, fresh air in at the front.

Desiccant

Section titled “Desiccant”

A desiccant soaks up water from the air, so the food dries faster without the air getting any hotter, and faster drying leaves less time for germs or pests. Silica gel can be dried out and used again. I designed a 3D-printed honeycomb that holds the beads and slides into the pipe: all the air in the loop passes through it, over a large surface of beads.

  • ⤢

    A honeycomb disc and a fine lattice over it, which keeps the beads in and lets the air through.

  • ⤢

    Silica beads spooned into the honeycomb on the printer's bed.

  • ⤢

    A finished disc, ready to slide into the pipe.

Power

Section titled “Power”

A 10 W solar panel runs the fan and charges the battery. The fan draws 12 V at 0.15 A, 21.6 Wh over a 12-hour day. To keep the battery’s depth of discharge low enough to last, and to keep going through cloudy days, we sized it at four 1.2 Ah lead-acid cells, 57.6 Wh: 38% depth of discharge in a day, and 2.5 days of running without sun.

Will Singapore's sun do?

A solar dryer is only useful if there’s sun when the food needs drying. We wanted to know how many days a year it couldn’t run, and what that costs in food.

Five years of sunlight

Section titled “Five years of sunlight”

We took Singapore’s global horizontal irradiance, the sunlight falling on flat ground, every half hour from 2016 to 2020 (from ReExplorer), and averaged it by month. March is the sunniest month; June and November are the dullest, November most of all.

  • ⤢

    Each year, month by month: every year dips in June and November.

  • ⤢

    The five-year average: highest in March, lowest in November.

To model the worst case, we took October to December, the three dullest months in a row: 92 days a year, 460 over the five years, enough to draw a conclusion from.

Days without enough sun

Section titled “Days without enough sun”

To run for a day, the dryer needs at least 400 Wh of sunlight on its mirrors. Rain and cloud come and go, so we counted a day as good if any two hours in it delivered that much onto the mirrors’ 0.42 m². Each day was then a yes or a no, so the count of bad days follows a binomial distribution.

  • 46 of the 460 days fell short: a proportion of 0.10.
  • The 95% confidence interval for that proportion, p ± 1.96 √(p(1 − p)/n), has an upper limit of 0.127.
  • Over a whole year, that’s no more than 47 days, about a month and a half, when the dryer can’t run. Since we used the dullest months for the whole year, the real number should be lower.

What it saves

Section titled “What it saves”

The dryer can take about 200 g of food a day.

  • The worst case: if strawberries (S$4.95 for 450 g at FairPrice) were waiting on all 47 bad days, 9.4 kg would spoil: S$103.4 a year.
  • The rest of the year: on the other 318 days it dries 63.6 kg of food. Even of a cheap fruit like bananas (S$2.45 for 700 g), that’s S$222.6.
  • So even counting the spoilt strawberries against the bananas, each dryer saves about S$119 a year.

It also costs nothing to run. An electric dryer using the same energy over those 318 days, 333.4 kWh, would cost S$90.68 a year at S$0.272 per kWh, so the S$145.5 prototype pays for itself in about 1.5 years on power alone, and in under half a year counting the food it saves. We left out the cost of maintenance, and of more drying capacity, as beyond this estimate.

Part 5 of 6

The roof test

We ran the prototype on a roof for 2 hours, with a wet sponge in the pipe standing in for the food. It drew 19 g of water out of the sponge.

What the data shows

Section titled “What the data shows”

The Arduino logged the air’s temperature and humidity, but it overheated after 30 minutes, so that’s all the data we have from it.

For the rest of the test we read the temperature now and then with a handheld data logger: it held steady at around 55 °C, inside the 45 to 60 °C target. For 15 minutes a building’s shadow fell on the dryer and the air dropped below 40 °C; once we moved it back into the sun, it came back up to the target.

Cost

Section titled “Cost”

Some parts came recycled from other projects (the aluminium profile and the acrylic ribs) and some were provided for the project (the solar panel, the Arduino, the fan, the sensor and the battery), and the aluminium tube only came in 6 m lengths, of which we used 1.5 m. So we estimated what the whole set-up would cost, prorating what we used: S$145.5, of which we actually spent S$99.

Part 6 of 6

Lessons

What we learnt

Section titled “What we learnt”
  • Keep the controller out of the heat. The Arduino overheated on the roof after 30 minutes and logged nothing more, so the rest of the test was read by hand.
  • Shade costs more than you’d think. Fifteen minutes in a building’s shadow dropped the air below 40 °C. Where it stands matters as much as how it’s built.
  • Mirrors made by hand lose light. Our film, glued by hand onto a plastic backing, didn’t hold a true parabola: the light reached the tubes as an uneven band, not a line. The plastic also scratched easily, and every scratch reflects less.

A next version

Section titled “A next version”
  • A heat battery. The mirrors collect far more heat on a sunny day than the drying needs. A heat exchanger could store the extra in a phase-change material under the mirrors, to keep drying on days without sun. Solar farms use molten salt; at this size, sodium acetate melts at a lower temperature and is safer and easier to work with.
  • Insulated tubes. A clear tube round each black one would keep its heat in, so the air gets hotter and rides out short dips in the sun.
  • A separate path through the desiccant, switched by the humidity, so the food dries fast while the desiccant needs drying out less often.
  • Better mirrors, made to a true parabola with a coating that doesn’t scratch, would concentrate more of the light, and dry more.
