The problem

Run a robotics competition that teaches undergraduates, half of them freshmen, to build a robot, on a playfield that starts and ends every match the same way.

Team

Team of 14: the organising committee, SUTD SOAR

A year after competing in RoboClash 2022, I led the committee that organised RoboClash 2023 (Trash Dash), the competition SUTD’s robotics club SOAR runs with LionsBot. 88 SUTD students took part, in teams of four, from 10 to 12 January 2023, and about half of them were freshmen. Besides leading the committee of 14 from May 2022, I designed the game and built its playfield and game elements, made the wireless system that starts and ends each match, wrote the StarterBot guide, and organised workshops that took participants through everything they needed to build their own robots.

Overview

The competition

Section titled “The competition”

RoboClash is the robotics competition SUTD’s robotics club SOAR runs with LionsBot. The 2023 edition, Trash Dash, ran from 10 to 12 January 2023 at SUTD’s Campus Centre. It was open to SUTD students in teams of four, and it was built to teach engineering by building a robot: about half of the 88 participants were freshmen. Every team got the same starter pack of parts.

The game

Section titled “The game”

I designed the game, Trash Dash, around sorting recyclables from trash. Two alliances of two teams, red and blue, played two-minute matches on a 3.5 m square playfield, raised on a 700 mm stage so the audience could see. Each match began with the balls dropping from 1 m above the centre and scattering, then ran 30 seconds of autonomous play and 90 seconds of driver control. Every robot had to start inside a 300 mm cube.

  • The recyclables were 100 red and blue floor balls, 72 mm across; the trash was 100 white foam balls, 50 mm across.
  • A ball in a recycling bin of its own colour scored 2 and a trash ball cost 1, but only once a robot had pushed the movable bin fully into a recycling zone, a circle 1.2 m across. In the 400 mm centre zone a bin’s points counted double, and the alliance with the most bins there got 8 more.
  • Any ball in one of the four trash bins scored 1. A bar across the middle of each trash bin made recyclables harder to throw away, as in real life.
  • The alliance that scored more in the autonomous period got 10 bonus points.
  • At the end, each robot touching the high charging bar scored 10, or 4 on the low one.

The rules changed across the game manual’s releases, from V1.0 in November 2022 to V2.1, as teams asked questions and the playfield took shape. Teams asked to use their own controllers, so any controller and processor became allowed. The autonomous bonus came in, and the charging tower turned 45° so its opening faced each alliance’s starting zone. Entanglement and shooting rules were relaxed in the last 10 seconds to help robots reach the charging bars, and the starting zones grew into 1200 by 500 mm acrylic sheets.

Section titled “The playfield and game elements”

I designed and built the playfield and the game elements: the movable recycling bins, the trash bins, the charging bars and the ball dispenser. By the competition, the dispenser stood at the centre of the playfield and released each match’s balls.

Starting and ending every match the same way

Section titled “Starting and ending every match the same way”

A master controller for the playfield talks to ESP32s over WLAN. At the start of a match it releases the balls from the dispenser; at the end it cuts power to every robot through a module on the 12 V LiPo each team ran its matches on. Every match starts and stops on the same signal for every team, and the robots stop where they are, so the final score is what’s on the field.

The StarterBot

Section titled “The StarterBot”

With half the participants in their first year, I wrote a build guide for a StarterBot: a robot base that drives, built from the starter pack, with CAD files and starter code. Teams built their own robots on it. The StarterBot guide shows how it goes together.

Workshops

Section titled “Workshops”

To go with the guide, I organised workshops for the participants on 3D modelling, electronics, fabrication, Arduino and programming: everything a team needed to get up and running and build its own robot.

From plan to match day

I competed in RoboClash 2022, and its participants’ feedback asked for three things: more variety in the game, more guidance and support, and a better starter kit. RoboClash 2023 was planned around those three, from the first preparations in May 2022 to the competition on 10 to 12 January 2023.

The plan

Section titled “The plan”

The game started as a pitch to sponsors: a 3.5 m square playfield themed on recycling, with tasks at three levels of difficulty. The easiest was pushing a bin into the centre zone; the middle one, putting trash into a bin; the hardest, sorting coloured recyclables from trash and touching an 80 cm charging bar at the end. From September 2022, a showcase booth outside the canteen put past robots and game elements in front of students, to draw them in before registration opened. Then we marked out the playfield on the floor of the room where it would be built.

Getting every team building

Section titled “Getting every team building”

About half the participants were freshmen, so the support was planned as carefully as the game. The plan: registration from late October to mid-November 2022, then a game brief where teams collected their starter kits and radios and saw the StarterBot they could build from them (the StarterBot guide). I organised a series of workshops on 3D modelling, electronics, fabrication, Arduino and programming, and in the weeks before the competition teams tested on the playfield and came to open consults for help.

Part 4 of 4My work

The StarterBot guide

About half of RoboClash 2023’s participants were freshmen, so every team got the same starter pack and I wrote a guide that turns it into a robot base that drives: the StarterBot. It was never meant to win. The guide says so at the start: it shows how the parts are used and gives a blueprint for a simple robot, and teams were expected to go well beyond it.

The guide (v1.0, 29 November 2022) has six parts: the components, hardware assembly, wiring, the code, radio control, and the motor drivers.

The starter pack

Section titled “The starter pack”

Every team got the same parts:

  • An Arduino Mega
  • Two 12 V geared DC motors and two BTS7960 motor drivers
  • Four MG996 servos for the team’s own mechanisms
  • A USB-C power delivery board, to run the robot from a power bank
  • Four 200 mm lengths of 2020 aluminium extrusion, a 200 by 200 mm acrylic sheet, brackets, fasteners and a set of custom 3D-printed mounts
  • A FlySky or Turnigy i6S radio, on loan

Robots could use up to 10 actuators. Teams could test at home on a power bank through the USB-C board, but every match ran on a 12 V LiPo handed out by the organisers, fitted with the module that cuts power at the end.

Building the base

Section titled “Building the base”

The guide builds the robot one part at a time, each step a render of the parts about to go together and the fasteners to use. The motor mounts and battery holder came 3D printed in the starter pack; for the electronics mounts, the guide links the CAD files and leaves the printing to the teams. Teams would rearrange the electronics around their own mechanisms anyway, so the guide recommends they design their own mounts for the space they end up with.

Wiring and power

Section titled “Wiring and power”

The wiring part starts with safety: take the power off before wiring, check every connection with a multimeter before switching on, and leave no bare copper, because a robot that failed the safety check before a match couldn’t play it. It then wires the robot for either power source: a power bank through the USB-C board, set to 12 V with the board’s push button, or a LiPo battery through a step-down converter that gives the Arduino and the receiver 5 V.

Code and radio

Section titled “Code and radio”

The example Arduino code drives the robot like a tank. The receiver sends all its channels to the Arduino on one wire (PPM); the left stick drives forwards and back, and the right stick steers, or turns the robot on the spot when the left stick is centred. Each BTS7960 driver takes a PWM signal on its enable pins for speed and two digital pins for direction, a wiring the guide admits is not intuitive, so the drivers get a part of their own with a table of pin states. The radio part shows how to set the transmitter to PPM and map its switches and knobs to spare channels, which teams could use to start and stop their autonomous sequence.
