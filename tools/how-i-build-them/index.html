How I build tools with AI

A lot of hardware work is bookkeeping around geometry: which connector goes where, how long each cable is, whether it can make the bend it’s asked to make, and what to order. It’s easy to get slightly wrong and expensive to find out late, on the bench. I build small tools for it with Claude. The planner on this site is a mockup of the one I use for a robot’s wiring, moved onto a home desk so it can be shared.

From CAD to cables, and back

Section titled “From CAD to cables, and back”

The tools pick up where the mechanical design leaves off. Once the parts are modelled, a Claude skill finds every place a cable plugs in and what that connector needs around it. The wiring diagram then says which of those ports connect, and each connection becomes a route that is checked, built into the CAD model and opened in the planner. The CAD model and the planner stay in step both ways: move a part in CAD and the planner picks it up, and a route reworked in the planner goes back to rebuild its cable in CAD.

How I split the work

Section titled “How I split the work”
  • I write the rules, as data. Parts are boxes with ports, and each port has the direction a cable leaves it. Each cable family has an outer diameter, a minimum bend radius and the straight length its connector needs. Keep-outs are boxes that must stay clear. The planner reads all of it from one file, so changing a rule never means changing code.
  • Claude writes most of the code. Route geometry, the checks, the tables and the 3D view are code Claude drafts and I review. I ask for the geometry and checks as plain functions with no screen attached, so a route can be tested on its own, and the same functions draw the page’s tables at build time and redraw them live as you edit.
  • I check the physics, not just the code. A passing check only helps if it’s checking the right thing: that a fillet sits where the cable really bends, that the straight is measured from the connector’s face, that the clash test ignores the part a cable plugs into but nothing else.
  • The checks decide. A route is right when it passes, not when it looks right, and a route the AI proposes goes through the same checks as mine.

The checks

Section titled “The checks”
Check What it looks at In the planner
Bend radius Each corner’s radius against the cable’s minimum Corners are filleted at 1.1 times the minimum, and shrink when two corners are too close to fit
Straight exit The run out of each connector before the first bend The connector’s boot and strain relief need it; a bend inside it is flagged
Clashes The route at its real diameter against every part Except the two parts it plugs into, near their connectors
Keep-outs Space that must stay clear The printer bed’s travel, front and back, and the knee space; a 50 mm grommet lets one cable through the desk
Length The route plus 5% for droop and service loops Against the length you’d buy; a lot of spare cable is a note, not a fault
USB power and data Whether each device reaches a computer, and whether every port gives the current drawn from it A hub without its own supply shares what one port gives it; the power strip’s USB ports only charge. This one is for the desk; on a robot it would be each power supply against its loads

Every route is made the same way: straight out of the connector, along runs that you move by dragging them along X, Y or Z, then straight into the other connector, with each corner filleted. Moving a run stretches its neighbours or adds a 45° jog, and it snaps to the things it can line up with: its connectors, other cables, a face of a part. The checks then run on the whole cable, sampled every 4 mm, while it moves.

A new cable starts the way it does at work: pick the port it starts at and the port it goes to, and only the ports it can reach with a cable you can buy light up. Then pick the cable, and Route it places it. The router tries every path with up to two moves along each axis through the lanes this desk offers (the tray, the gap behind the desk, the desk top, the floor, the grommet, past the front edge), lets a run slant where two ports sit a few millimetres apart, and keeps the route with the fewest problems, then the shortest. It takes about 20 milliseconds in your browser and calls no AI; slide a device and its cables are routed again as it moves.

The AI parts

Section titled “The AI parts”

The planner on this site never calls Claude. The work version can: from a one-line hint (“through the tray, clear of the bed”) it proposes a route, which then goes through the same checks as one I draw. Here, the Auto-route button stays off, and a run I recorded while building the demo stands in for it: the first route for the printer’s power cord crossed the bed’s travel, and the proposed fix clears it with every bend above the cord’s minimum. You can apply it, check it and undo it.

What this copy simplifies

Section titled “What this copy simplifies”
  • Cable data are rules of thumb, such as a minimum bend of about four to five times the outer diameter, not values from datasheets.
  • Devices draw typical currents (a 300 mA backlit keyboard, a 900 mA portable SSD), and a port gives what its USB version allows.
  • Route it can’t be steered. The work version also lets me click where a route should pass, and how tightly, before it routes, or draw the corners myself; here you drag the runs afterwards.
  • Parts are boxes. The clash check works on boxes, not CAD geometry, and cables don’t check against each other.
  • The desk stands in for a robot. The work version follows a robot’s power wiring, and its parts, routes and rules stay private.
