Try the screw-access checker

Every screw in a robot has to go in at some point in the build, with a tool, and with a hand round that tool. A screw the CAD model is happy with can still be one nobody can reach: under a motor that went on first, behind a heatsink, at the bottom of a tray too deep for a driver’s handle. This checker goes through an assembly the way I would. It finds the screws from the holes (a clearance hole leading into a tapped one), counts them by size and length, then for each one tries a hex driver straight in, with room for a hand round it, against only the parts that are there when that screw goes in. If the driver can’t reach, it tries a long driver, a ball-end driver tilted up to 25° and an L-shaped hex key. Each screw comes out good to go, hard to assemble or impossible. The robot is a mockup, not one I’ve worked on: a small indoor robot’s base, built in ten steps, with five problems planted in it.

    Try this: find the planted problems
    1. Read the result. The line across the top of the view counts the screws, and the groups beside it say what happens to each: impossible first, then hard, then good to go. Click a group, or a marker on the view, to see that screw as it goes in: only the parts that are there then, the driver and the room your hand needs drawn along it, red where they hit, and the tool that works instead. ‹ and › step through the screws.
    2. Under the drive motors. Each drive module is built on the bench, then fitted to the base plate at step 2. Two of each bracket’s four screws sit under its motor, 5 mm below it, so no driver, ball-end or hex key gets in. Fitted before the motor, they’d be fine.
    3. Beside the battery tray’s wall. Two of the tray’s floor screws are 6 mm from its 92 mm back wall. A driver’s blade fits, but its handle hits the wall: only a 150 mm driver reaches.
    4. Under the heatsink. One of the main board’s corner screws sits under the heatsink’s fins, which come on the board. A ball-end driver tilted 25° gets past, but can’t tighten it fully.
    5. The LiDAR from below. The LiDAR is held by four screws from under the top deck, where the main board leaves no room for a driver and your hand. Only a hex key fits.
    6. Behind the E-stop. The rear panel comes with its E-stop on, and the mushroom head sits 5 mm out from one of the panel’s screws. Nothing reaches it.
    7. Play the build to watch the robot go together a step at a time, or pick a step under Build.
    8. Change the tools you allow. Untick the hex key and the LiDAR’s screws become impossible. Give your hand more room and more screws turn hard.
    9. Open your own model, an STL or a GLB. It stays in your browser: nothing is uploaded or saved.

      How it checks

      Finding the screws. A round hole in a flat face, 1 to 20 mm across, is an opening. Openings on one axis line up into a stack of holes through parts that touch. A clearance hole leading into a tapped one is a screw, its head on the clearance side; a tapped part between two clearance holes takes a screw from each end; two clearance holes with nothing tapped are a bolt and a nut. The size is the metric size whose clearance and tapped holes match, and the length the shortest standard one that reaches 1.5 times its size into the tapped part, short of a blind hole's floor. They're taken as socket head cap screws (ISO 4762).

      When. Each screw is checked with only the parts that are there when it goes in: on the mockup, as the build goes; on your model, at the step you give each part.

      • Good to go: a hex driver goes straight in. Its blade is 75 mm long (100 mm from the 3 mm hex up), its handle 26 mm across and 100 mm long, with room round the handle for your hand (90 mm across, settable above) and your forearm behind it.
      • Hard to assemble: only a long driver (150 mm blade) reaches, or a ball-end driver tilted up to 25°, or an L-shaped hex key with room to turn at least 60° a stroke, its short or long end in the screw. Hex keys are ISO 2936 sizes: 2 mm, 50 by 16 mm; 2.5 mm, 56 by 18 mm; 3 mm, 63 by 20 mm; 4 mm, 70 by 25 mm.
      • Impossible: none of them reaches, or the head itself has no room.

      Not checked: how tight a screw goes, whether you can see it, holding a part while you drive it, nuts and washers, screws that form their own thread in plastic, and hands of other shapes. A screw that's good to go here can still be awkward, and one marked hard or impossible is worth a second look in the CAD.

      Your own model. Open an STL or a GLB, or drop it on the view, then set its units and which way is up. Its screws come from its holes, so a model that has its holes the way a CAD model does works best: round holes in flat faces, a clearance hole in one part over a tapped hole in the next, modelled at the tap drill’s size or the screw’s. A model has no build order, so every part starts at step 1 and each screw is checked with the whole assembly there. Give a cover, or anything else that goes on later, a later step, and it’s left out for the screws that go in before it. Holes that line up with nothing to screw into are counted, not checked.

      Your tool settings stay in this browser until you reset them, and nothing is sent anywhere. The checker loads about 200 KB and checks the mockup’s 74 screws in about half a second, in the background, so the view keeps turning.
