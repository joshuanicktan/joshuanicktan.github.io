Try the water-path simulator

A robot that cleans floors carries water inside it and gets splashed from outside, and the place to find out where that water goes is the CAD model, before anything is built. This simulator follows thousands of drops over a robot vacuum-mop built the way a real one is assembled: a cover with a rounded edge on a body ring with a lip-and-groove joint, a bumper round the front half that moves, a door for the water tank, a LiDAR turret under its cap, a lid over the tank and two buttons on top, and underneath, the wheels in their wells, a side brush and the mop pad. It runs rain and spray on the shell and a leaking joint inside, then checks three things: whether anything that must stay dry gets wet, where water gets in, and whether what gets in can drain. The robot is a mockup, not one I’ve worked on, with eight design faults planted for you to find.

Try this: find and fix the eight planted faults
  1. Read the result. The line across the top of the view says what’s wrong, and each check beside it says how the water gets there and how to fix it. Click a check to play just the water behind it, or press Apply on its fix and watch it clear. Amber circles mark where water gets in, red squares where it reaches a part that must stay dry, and harmless runoff fades out.
  2. Water gets in round the LiDAR turret. Pick Spray, like IPX3 and watch the drops that run into the gap round the turret turn amber, then red on the main board. Turn on A skirt round the LiDAR turret and the gap stays dry.
  3. The cover’s joint is the wrong way round. The body’s lip stands outside the cover, so the groove between them catches the water and runs it in, all the way round. Turn on Flip the cover’s joint: the cover’s skirt now laps over the lip and drips clear.
  4. Now the tank door leaks. The flipped cover sheds its water down the back of the body, onto the top of the tank door and in at its seam. Turn on A drip edge over the tank door.
  5. The bumper’s gap is open at the top. Water dripping off the body lands on the bumper and runs in behind it. Turn on A drip lip over the front bumper.
  6. The lid’s seam is open. Rain, and the water running down the cover, fall into the seam round the lid over the tank. Turn on A gasket round the lid: it seals the seam flush, and the water runs over it.
  7. The buttons leak onto the main board. Water runs into the gaps round the two buttons in front of the turret and drips onto the board under them. Turn on A membrane over the buttons.
  8. The tank’s joint weeps. Pick The tank outlet’s joint weeps: the water runs down the pump cable into the board’s connector. Drag the square on the cable down until it says “a drip loop”, and the water drips into the drain basin instead.
  9. The pump’s joint sprays the battery. Pick The pump outlet’s joint sprays, then turn on A splash guard by the pump.
  10. Click anywhere on the robot to drip water there, or press Spray and click to hose it from where you’re looking.
  11. Open your own model, an STL or a GLB. It stays in your browser: nothing is uploaded or saved.

      How the water moves

      Each drop is followed on its own. It checks where water goes, not how much: it's a path checker, not a flow simulation.

      • A drop falls under gravity until it hits something, then runs straight downhill along the surface.
      • On a surface flatter than 1.5°, it spreads the way it was moving until it finds a slope or an edge.
      • Under an overhang it clings up to 60° past vertical, then drips. On cables and hoses it runs along the underside unless they're within 2° of level.
      • A turn sharper than 40° onto an underside is a drip edge: the drop falls from there.
      • A drop that can't get any lower pools; under an overhang it grows and drips instead.
      • It runs down a vertical wall at 0.25 m/s and slower on gentler slopes. That only sets which part gets wet first.

      The presets roughly follow the setups in IEC 60529: drips straight down (IPX1), drips with the robot tilted 15° each of four ways (IPX2), spray up to 60° from vertical (IPX3) and splashing from every direction (IPX4). They show where water goes, not the test's flow rates or times, so passing here isn't an IP rating.

      Not modelled: how much water there is, splashes off a surface, water drawn into hairline gaps, and gaps smaller than the model shows.

      Your own model. Open an STL or a GLB, or drop it on the view, then set its units and which way is up. An STL splits into its separate bodies, and a GLB keeps its part names. Mark the parts that must stay dry and the cables and hoses, and add leaks at fittings with Leak. The same rain, spray and checks run on it. Which faces count as inside is worked out from what each face can see out of the model, so a deep pocket under a lip can count as inside; Inside faces under Show colours them so you can check. On your model, water counts as getting in once it stays in, reaches a part that must stay dry, or runs 15 mm inside, and since its gaps have no names, each is given by where it is, joined up along the seam.

      Your settings for the mockup stay in this browser until you reset them, and nothing is sent anywhere. The simulator loads about 220 KB and runs the drops in the background, about 4,300 of them in one to two seconds, so the view keeps turning while they run.
