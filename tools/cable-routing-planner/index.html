Try the cable-routing planner

This is a mockup. At work I plan a robot’s power wiring and harnesses with a tool I built with Claude, but I can’t share the robot or its data. So this copy of the tool works on a home desk instead: a PC, a monitor, a 3D printer, and a Raspberry Pi wired to blink an LED. On a robot the steps are the same, with many more parts and cables in a tighter space. You place the parts and their connectors, route each cable, check every bend, clearance and length, then read off the lengths and parts to order. Adding a device works the way adding a part to the robot does: pick the port its cable starts at and the port it goes to, pick the cable, and the router places it.

How to edit

  • Select a cable: click it (anywhere near it counts), or its row under Checks or in the schedule. Ctrl+click (⌘ on a Mac) adds more. Esc lets go, one step at a time.
  • By what it connects to: click a part (the PC, the monitor, a device) and it lights up with its cables and the parts at their other ends; click one of those cables to select it. U adds everything connected to the selection, I isolates it.
  • Right-click a cable, a part or empty space for its options: near a cable is enough, and what the menu is for stands out while it's open (long-press on a touch screen, or ⋯ on its card, or Shift+F10). A right-drag pans instead.
  • Move a run: drag a square on the selected cable (or the cable itself). The run slides along X, Y or Z, whichever matches your drag; its neighbours stretch, or take a 45° jog.
  • While dragging: X, Y or Z locks the axis · Shift for 0.5 mm steps and no snapping · Alt for square jogs · type a number for an exact distance, Enter to apply · Esc cancels.
  • It snaps to things it can line up with: its connectors, other cables, 3 mm off a part, and the run beyond a jog (the jog then disappears).
  • Bend point: double-click the selected cable, then drag a square on either side of the dot.
  • After a move, its distance stays editable as a number in the cable's card.
  • View: drag to turn it, right-drag or middle-drag to pan, scroll to zoom. Full screen fills the window; Esc, ✕ or Back returns. On a touch screen, open the planner full screen: one finger turns, two pan, pinch zooms, double-tap frames.
  • Plug in puts a device on the desk: a USB-C keyboard, a mouse, a USB hub, an SSD, a webcam or a lamp. Drag it to slide it; its cables re-route as it moves.
  • New cable: click the port it starts at, then the port it goes to (ports that can't take it are dimmed), pick the cable, and Route it. In the wiring diagram you can also drag from one port to another.
  • Unplug an added cable, or remove a device, from its card beside the view.
  • Show me, on each Try this step, points at what to do next and moves on as you do it.
  • Undo with Ctrl+Z, redo with Ctrl+Y. Edits stay in this browser.

Try this

  1. W6, the HDMI cable, bends too tightly where it goes into the monitor. Click it, then drag the square on its short upright run behind the monitor back toward the wall, against the blue +Z arrow that shows when you point at the square. The bend opens up and the check clears.
  2. W4, the printer’s power cord, has two problems: it climbs through the printer bed’s travel, and it needs more cord than it has. Drag the square on its upright run behind the printer toward the monitor until the step in it disappears, or apply Claude’s route under Auto-route.
  3. The LED runs at 3.9 mA through a 330 Ω resistor. Pick 100 Ω and the current check flags it.
  4. Plug in a keyboard. Under Plug in, add the USB-C keyboard, then pick a USB port that lights up (a numbered dot lists the ports on that side of a part), choose the cable and press Route it. To move the keyboard, pick it under Plug in again and drag the square on it: its cable re-routes, and “Shortest that fits” picks a new length.
  5. Overload a hub. Add the USB hub and plug it into one of the PC’s USB-A ports. Unplug the keyboard (select its cable, then Unplug), then plug the keyboard, the mouse and the SSD into the hub. Together they draw more than one USB port can give, so the USB check flags the hub’s cable. Unplug the SSD, give it its own port on the PC, and the check clears.

The LED

GPIO17 (pin 11) drives a red LED through a resistor on the breadboard. The Pi runs:

from gpiozero import LED
from signal import pause

led = LED(17)
led.blink(on_time=0.5, off_time=0.5)
pause()

I = (3.3 V − 2.0 V) ÷ 330 Ω = 3.9 mA

OK Comfortable for a Pi pin (aim for 8 mA or less). The resistor dissipates 5.1 mW.
