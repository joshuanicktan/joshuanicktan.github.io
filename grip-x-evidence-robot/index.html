Part 2 of 5

Overview

The manual process, the three-station system our team of 7 built, how it performed, and my part in it.

The problem

Section titled “The problem”

HTX’s Robotics, Automation and Unmanned Systems Centre of Expertise builds robots for the Home Team. The process it brought us was manual from start to finish. An officer picks out each item and arranges it on a table, climbs a step ladder to photograph it from above, transfers the photos and pairs each one with its description and dimensions, then weighs the item and seals it in a bag. It’s tedious and repetitive, and over time it causes fatigue and risks injury.

Existing sorting robots suit low-mix, high-volume work with fixed spacing and known objects. Here every item is different. Our brief: cut the officer’s time to 10% while still categorising and documenting every item accurately.

What we built

Section titled “What we built”

Grip-X works across three stations, with a UR5 arm and our gripper moving each object between them:

  1. Unsorted station: the officer puts down a tray of items and starts the program. A depth camera and our grasping algorithm find each object and plan a grip, even in a cluttered tray.
  2. Photo booth: three cameras photograph the object. Software estimates its size and an OpenAI vision model writes a description, both saved to a database.
  3. Sorted station: the arm places the object in one of two trays by size.

The officer can leave it running and collect the sorted, documented items afterwards. A touch-screen interface runs it, so nobody has to use a command line or ROS.

How it performed

Section titled “How it performed”
  • 10% of the manual time at 4 objects, and less for larger batches.
  • 20 of 23 different object types processed reliably, end to end.
  • 85% of objects sorted into the right size group by the team’s vision software, with sizes within ±12 mm.

My part

Section titled “My part”

I was the hardware designer in a team of seven from three pillars (three of us in Engineering Product Development, two in Engineering Systems and Design, two in Computer Science and Design).

  • I designed the adaptive gripper: a reverse double four-bar linkage that holds 1.75 kg.
  • I built the photo booth that photographs each object from three angles.

Part 3 of 5My work

Adaptive gripper

A reverse double four-bar gripper on a DC motor that holds 1.75 kg, beyond its 1 kg target, and opens for objects up to 200 mm wide.

The gripper had to pick up objects of different shapes, sizes and surfaces from a cluttered tray, one after another. I designed it to carry objects up to 200 mm wide and 1 kg in weight.

A reverse double four-bar

Section titled “A reverse double four-bar”

The fingers sit on a reverse double four-bar (RD4B): two four-bar linkages geared to move in opposite directions, so the jaws open wide while staying parallel. A DC motor drives it, and its high torque lets the gripper hold objects up to 1.75 kg reliably, well beyond the 1 kg target.

Position, then force

Section titled “Position, then force”

As the bar linkages rotate, the centre of the pads moves up and down a little, which would throw off a grasp. So the gripper works in two steps. First a position control loop, reading the rotation sensor, opens the jaws to the width of the object. Then a pressure control mode, reading the force-sensitive resistor, squeezes just hard enough to hold it, so objects don’t slip.

Part 4 of 5My work

Photo booth

A three-camera booth that photographs every object the same way, so the system can size it within ±12 mm and describe it with AI.

The officer’s old way of documenting an item was to climb a step ladder and photograph it from above. I built the photo booth that replaces that step: the arm places each object inside, and three cameras, one above and two at the front, photograph it from fixed positions every time.

From photos to a size

Section titled “From photos to a size”

The cameras are fixed, so their lenses and positions can be calibrated. The team’s software compares each photo with an empty background shot, finds the object’s outline, and combines the three views into a bounding box with real dimensions.

85% of objects were sorted into the right size group, with sizes within ±12 mm.

From photos to a description

Section titled “From photos to a description”

The three views are combined into one collage and sent to OpenAI’s vision model, which writes a description of the object for the database alongside its size and the time it was recorded.

Part 5 of 5

Results and the live demo

Under 10% of the manual time from 4 objects on, 20 of 23 object types handled, and a live origami challenge from SUTD's president.

How it performed

Section titled “How it performed”

We measured the system against the brief: cut the officer’s time to 10% while still sorting and documenting accurately.

  • Time: 10% of the manual time at 4 objects, and less for larger batches.
  • Range: 20 of 23 different object types processed reliably, end to end.
  • Sorting: 85% of objects sorted into the right size group by the team’s vision software, with sizes within ±12 mm.

The live origami challenge

Section titled “The live origami challenge”

In February 2025, SUTD’s president, Professor Phoon Kok Kwang, invited our team to show Grip-X to visiting guests before his talk. On the spot, he asked a random member of the audience to fold any origami object, and challenged the gripper to pick it up.

The gripper picked up every object the audience folded except one.
