The problem

Floor robots can scrub a restroom floor, but toilets, urinals, sinks and litter need arms that reach from floor to mirror, in rooms built for people.

Team

Team of 13: me and 12 hardware interns over two cycles, with the autonomy and AI teams

This is the dual-arm prototype of LionsBot T1, a restroom-cleaning robot from Griffin Labs, LionsBot’s embodied-AI research arm: two arms on a tall lift, on a swerve-drive base, that clean from the floor to the mirror. The teleoperation and data-collection setup I built on it fed the team’s VLA training, the work that led to Griffin Alpha, and I now lead the hardware of its single-arm successor.

Overview

What T1 does in a restroom, how it grew from a bare swerve base in September 2025 to three Interclean units in April 2026, and who built it.

What it does

Section titled “What it does”

T1 is built for the hardest room in the building to clean. In LionsBot’s public description, it maps a restroom by itself, picks up litter and scrubs surfaces with its two arms, and sanitises its own tools after every task so nothing carries from a toilet to a sink. It cleans with ozonated water rather than chemicals: a mist to disinfect surfaces and a jet for stubborn stains. Its battery swaps out, and is the same battery as LionsBot’s R5 floor scrubber, so it can run around the clock.

When the robot meets something it can’t handle, a person can take over. In T1’s award pitch, LionsBot describes this human-in-the-loop teleoperation as a cleaner stepping in remotely to manage edge cases.

My role and the team

I led the hardware team. I set T1’s architecture, then led 12 interns over two internship cycles, about eight months, to build three robots. Modules that carry over

Section titled “Modules that carry over”

I plan the hardware as modules with clear mechanical, power and data interfaces between them, so a module can be reused rather than redesigned. The aim goes beyond one robot: a later robot, on a different platform, should start from proven components instead of from scratch, and come together faster.

Floor to mirror

Section titled “Floor to mirror”

A restroom asks for work at every height: litter on the floor, the inside of a toilet bowl, a urinal, a countertop, a mirror. Instead of a longer arm, T1 moves both arms up and down a lift, so the arms stay compact and work close to the robot at any height.

Swerve-drive base

The mobile base I owned end to end: a swerve drive that moves T1 in any direction in tight restrooms, from first draft in September 2025 to three units.

I owned the base end to end: the first drafts, the design, procurement and the build of all three units.

Why swerve

Section titled “Why swerve”

Restrooms are tight: stalls, rows of sinks and urinals, doors. A swerve drive steers every wheel, so T1 can move sideways or diagonally without turning on the spot first, and line up square to a fixture. It also has to carry the battery, the power system, the compute, the lift and both arms, and stay stable with the arms out and the lift raised.

From first draft to the Interclean base

Section titled “From first draft to the Interclean base”

The first draft of the base and its swerve modules came in early September 2025, and the first mobile base later that month. A second revision of the base followed in November and December 2025, and it carried T1 through to the three Interclean units.

Battery swap

Section titled “Battery swap”

T1 uses the same swappable battery as LionsBot’s R5 floor scrubber. Swapping a flat battery for a charged one, instead of waiting for a charge, is what lets the robot run 24/7. LionsBot’s brochure sums it up as “Swappable battery. No downtime.”

Power system

Section titled “Power system”

One battery feeds everything on the robot: the swerve drive, the lift, two arms, the sensors and the compute.

Why T1 needs a person in the loop

Section titled “Why T1 needs a person in the loop”

A restroom is messy and never quite the same twice, so T1 is designed for a person to take over when it meets something it can’t handle: in LionsBot’s words, a cleaner can remotely step in to manage edge cases. That is what lets it go to work before its AI can do everything alone; the brochure’s headline is “Grasps anything. Day one deployment.”, with a “human pilot in the loop”. The same setup serves the AI team: every task a person teleoperates is a demonstration, and demonstrations are what a vision-language-action (VLA) model learns from. The launch video of Griffin Alpha, the model the AI team built next, opens on T1 (what came next).

Designing the robot and its data together

Section titled “Designing the robot and its data together”

A model learns the body it was recorded on. Where the cameras sit and what they see, how the arms move, what the grippers can hold and how the operator’s controls map onto the joints all end up in the data. Data recorded on one body doesn’t carry over cleanly to a different one, so the recording can’t be an afterthought bolted onto a finished robot.

That’s why I treat data recording as part of the hardware design: camera placement, the teleoperation mapping and what gets recorded are decided alongside the mechanical design, not after it. On T1, I designed the teleoperation and data-collection setup together with the robot itself.
