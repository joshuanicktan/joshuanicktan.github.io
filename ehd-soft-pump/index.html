The problem

Soft robots need small, silent pumps. EHD pumps have no moving parts, but discharges and uneven fabrication limit their reliability and power.

A three-month research placement in 2024 in the Maeda Lab (Smart Materials and Robotics) at Tokyo Institute of Technology, now Institute of Science Tokyo. The lab works on electrohydrodynamic (EHD) pumps, which move a liquid with an electric field and have no moving parts, so they can be small and silent enough for soft robots. I developed a way to make wire-electrode pumps with nothing but a laser cutter, raised their power density by 50%, and measured how they performed.

The work fed into a paper I co-authored: A Compact, Self-Recovering Wire Electrode Electrohydrodynamic Pump for High-Speed McKibben Artificial Muscle Actuation, Advanced Intelligent Systems, 2025. The 25 × 25 × 5 mm figures are from my own prototypes; the rest of these pages comes from the paper.

Overview

How an EHD pump works, what I did in three months at the Maeda Lab, and the 2025 paper it fed into: 107 kPa, 88 mL/min and a tenfold faster artificial muscle.

How an EHD pump works

Section titled “How an EHD pump works”

An electrohydrodynamic pump moves an insulating liquid with an electric field. A high voltage across pairs of electrodes injects charge into the liquid, and the field pulls the charged liquid along, dragging the rest with it. Only the liquid moves, so the pump is silent and can be very small. That suits soft robots, which often run on fluid pressure but usually need a bulky, noisy pump to supply it.

The catch is reliability. If the liquid breaks down electrically between the electrodes, the pump can stop, and pumps that are hard to make consistently vary from one unit to the next.

The paper

Section titled “The paper”

The work fed into A Compact, Self-Recovering Wire Electrode Electrohydrodynamic Pump for High-Speed McKibben Artificial Muscle Actuation (Advanced Intelligent Systems, 2025), which I co-authored with Amr Marzuq, Yu Kuwajima and colleagues in the lab. The final pump in the paper uses 0.1 mm copper wire electrodes set diagonally in a laser-cut acrylic frame. It reached 107 kPa and 88 mL/min, double the power density of the lab’s previous pump, and kept 88% of its flow after 50 discharges, recovering automatically after each one.

Paired with a McKibben artificial muscle, it contracted the muscle in 2 s, ten times faster than the lab’s earlier EHD-driven system.

Part 3 of 4My work

Wire electrodes made with a laser cutter

A scalable way to make wire-electrode EHD pumps with only a laser cutter: 20 minutes and about S$0.80 of material per pump, down from 3 hours.

I developed a way to make the pumps that needs nothing but an inexpensive CO2 laser cutter and costs about S$0.80 of material per pump. Fine wire electrodes replace printed or etched ones, so there’s no lithography, and the laser-cut acrylic layers hold them straight and evenly spaced. Alignment notches in the layers let a pump be put together by hand, and making one takes 20 minutes instead of 3 hours.

The problem with the old pump

Section titled “The problem with the old pump”

The lab’s previous pump used copper-sheet electrodes, set diagonally in flexible layers, and survived 100 discharges with 90% of its performance. But it was cut on a cutting plotter, which can’t make an electrode narrower than about 1 mm, so only so many electrode pairs fit along a channel, and power density stayed low. Its flexible base also bulged under pressure, which distorted the channel and spread the electrodes apart, and wider channels pumped much worse because of it. Wire electrodes 0.1 mm across, in a rigid acrylic frame, fix both: many more pairs per millimetre, and a channel that keeps its shape.

Three layers

Section titled “Three layers”

The process, as the paper describes it:

  1. Laser cut each 2 mm acrylic plate in three passes at different power and speed: engrave the groove for the copper strip, engrave the wire path, then cut the outline, including guide teeth along the edges.
  2. Fill the wire grooves with a little UV-curing solder-mask ink.
  3. Wind 0.1 mm copper wire (99.9% pure) around the plate, using the guide teeth to keep the spacing.
  4. Join the wires on each side with a copper strip with conductive adhesive, and trim the excess.
  5. Cure the ink under UV light for 5 minutes to lock the wires in place.
  6. Bond the two plates with 0.5 mm 3M VHB tape, precut to form the channel, and fit the tube connectors.

The method scales by changing the cut file: more electrode pairs for more pressure, a wider channel for more flow. Across five pumps made the same way, the coefficient of variation was under 5% for flow and under 2% for pressure.

Part 4 of 4My work

Performance and repeatability

P-Q curves across voltages and electrode configurations in LabVIEW: 40 mL/min and 20 kPa at 8 kV from my prototype, and 50% more power density.

I characterised the pumps’ pressure-flow (P-Q) curves across operating voltages and electrode configurations, with the measurements run in LabVIEW. My best prototype, 25 × 25 × 5 mm, reached 40 mL/min and 20 kPa at 8 kV, and the wire electrodes raised power density by 50%.

Sweeping the electrode layout

Section titled “Sweeping the electrode layout”

I varied the electrode spacing and offset in 0.05 mm steps from one pump to the next. Testing each step let us check the simulation results against real pumps and find the best layouts for high pressure and for high flow.

The test rig

Section titled “The test rig”

As the paper describes it, the pump draws a dielectric fluid (3M Novec 7300, chosen for its very low conductivity) from a reservoir, past a pressure sensor, a flow sensor and a motorised pinch valve. A high-voltage amplifier drives the pump, and a LabVIEW program sets the voltage, moves the valve and logs pressure, flow and current.

  • Pressure and flow against voltage: the voltage climbs in 0.5 kV steps of 30 s each until the liquid breaks down, and each point is the average over its step. The valve is shut to measure maximum pressure and fully open to measure maximum flow.
  • P-Q curves: at a fixed voltage, the valve closes from fully open to shut over 10 minutes, tracing the curve from maximum flow to maximum pressure. Then the next voltage.
