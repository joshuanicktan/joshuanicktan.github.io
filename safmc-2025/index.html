The problem

Drones that pick up payloads with no one touching them, flown through a maze by one pilot with wearables instead of joysticks.

Team

Team of 5: SUTD students

In 2025, SUTD’s team B-LUP won the Category D1 Championship Award at the Singapore Amazing Flying Machine Competition (SAFMC), the drone competition run by DSO National Laboratories and Science Centre Singapore. I worked on the mechanical design and electronics.

The mission

Section titled “The mission”

Category D1 is Man-Machine Teaming. One pilot flies up to three drones from a booth with no line of sight to them, using a video link and wearable controls such as gloves: anything gripped, like a joystick, is banned. Each drone takes off from the team’s launchpad, picks up a red or green beanbag from the team’s supply zone, flies a walled maze or a shortcut through three windows, and drops the beanbag, in the air, onto a lit tile of the same colour in a three-by-three grid. A tile turns white once it’s scored, and the drone flies back for another. Each run lasts 10 minutes, and after qualifying, teams meet head to head on grids that are linked, so a tile one team scores is gone for the other. The mission is 40% of the result; judges score the rest on the aircraft, its creativity, the team’s learning journey and a challenge video.

The ground station

Section titled “The ground station”

The rules let each team build its own supply zone, with one catch: no one may touch a payload once the run starts, so the drone has to load itself. The payloads start spread around a 1 m circle on the floor.

Our ground station is a 1 m disc with a slot for each payload carrier around its edge. The blue mast is there to help the pilot: they fly the drone into it, then lower the throttle, and the drone lands on the blue pyramid in the middle, which centres it. The station then reels the carriers in from the edge to the drone, where magnets hold them, and the drone lifts off with them. Each carrier holds one beanbag and is red or green, as the rules require.

The drone

Section titled “The drone”

A carbon-fibre quadcopter, inside the rules’ 2 kg and 1 m, with its battery strapped on top and pink guards on two of its propellers. The payload carriers hang by magnets around a drum under its centre. Over a tile of the right colour, the drum turns and a cam knocks one carrier off, so the drone can drop its payloads one at a time on different tiles in a single trip, and each extra payload delivered on the same trip scores a bonus point.

To drop from the right height, the pilot has two lasers. They point down at angles that meet at the best drop height, so when the two dots on the floor become one, the drone is there.
